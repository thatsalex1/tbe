5 Common Crypto Escrow Scams to Avoid

By the LegacyHold Team · 5 min read

Escrow exists to make crypto deals safer, but the word "escrow" itself has become a tool scammers borrow to sound trustworthy. Here are the five patterns we see most often, and how to spot each one before it costs you.

1The "move off-platform" request

You start a deal through a legitimate escrow service, and the other party asks to finish it over Telegram, WhatsApp, or email instead — usually with a reason that sounds plausible, like "lower fees" or "it's faster." Once you're off-platform, there's no escrow, no dispute process, and no record of what was agreed.

Protect yourself: Keep every part of the deal, including the payment itself, inside the platform. A legitimate counterparty has no reason to insist on leaving it.

2Clone and typo-squatted websites

Scammers copy a real escrow site pixel-for-pixel onto a domain that's one letter off from the original, then send you a link via DM or a fake invoice. The wallet address on that clone site belongs to them, not to any real escrow wallet.

Protect yourself: Type the domain yourself or use a bookmark instead of clicking links sent by a counterparty, and double-check the URL in your address bar before you ever enter a wallet address.

3Fake "support agents" asking for your seed phrase

No legitimate escrow service, including LegacyHold, will ever ask for your wallet's private key or seed phrase. That's not how custody works here — we only need a deposit address, never the keys to your personal wallet. Anyone who asks for your seed phrase to "verify" your deposit or "unlock" your funds is attempting to drain your wallet directly.

Protect yourself: Treat any request for your seed phrase as an automatic red flag, no matter how official it looks.

4"Send a test transaction first" requests

A counterparty asks you to send a small "test" amount directly to their wallet before the real escrow deposit, to "confirm the address works." There's no technical reason this is ever necessary — it's a way to extract a free payment from you with no protection at all.

Protect yourself: Only ever send funds to the actual escrow deposit address provided by the platform, never to a counterparty's personal wallet, test or otherwise.

5Pressure to release funds early

After you've funded escrow, the seller pushes hard for you to confirm receipt and release the funds before you've actually verified the goods, service, or asset — often citing a ticking deadline or an "urgent" reason. Once crypto is released, it can't be pulled back.

Protect yourself: Take the time you need to verify the deal before confirming. A legitimate seller can wait the extra few minutes.

The common thread

Every one of these scams works by getting you to step outside the protection escrow actually provides — off-platform, off-domain, or ahead of verification. As long as the deal, the payment, and the confirmation all stay inside a legitimate escrow flow, the structural protection holds.
